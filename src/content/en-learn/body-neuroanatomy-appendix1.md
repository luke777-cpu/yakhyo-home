# Appendix 1. Parkinson's Disease and Parkinsonism — What Is the Difference?

## — They look alike, but they are not the same disease

The name Parkinson's disease is familiar. A disease where the hand trembles, the body stiffens, movement slows, and the stride shortens.

But in the clinic, or in a medical textbook, another similar-sounding term shows up. **Parkinsonism.**

Here is the most important difference.

> **Parkinson's disease is one specific disease. Parkinsonism is a clinical term for a range of conditions that share similar motor symptoms.**

---

## 1. Parkinsonism Is a Bundle of Symptoms

Parkinsonism does not refer to a single disease. It describes a state in which parkinsonian motor symptoms appear — bradykinesia, rigidity, rest tremor, and postural instability.

In other words, a patient presenting with similar features — slow, stiff, shaking, walking with difficulty — is not necessarily someone with Parkinson's disease. Several diseases, each with a different cause and pathological mechanism, can produce similar motor symptoms.

## 2. Parkinson's Disease Sits Inside Parkinsonism

Parkinson's disease is the most representative cause of parkinsonism.

> Parkinson's disease sits inside the larger box called parkinsonism.

Parkinson's disease is a degenerative disease mainly associated with the loss of dopamine neurons in the substantia nigra. As a result, dopamine in the striatum decreases, the balance of the basal ganglia circuit breaks down, and bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor, and motor fluctuations appear.

## 3. Other Diseases That Produce Similar Symptoms

There are diseases that produce parkinsonian motor symptoms without being Parkinson's disease. The most representative are Multiple System Atrophy (MSA), Progressive Supranuclear Palsy (PSP), Corticobasal Syndrome (CBS), and Dementia with Lewy Bodies (DLB). These are often called **atypical parkinsonism**.

## 4. The Response to Levodopa Is a Very Important Clue

One of the most important features of Parkinson's disease is its **response to levodopa**. In typical Parkinson's disease, bradykinesia, rigidity, and some tremor can improve considerably with levodopa.

In atypical parkinsonism, by contrast, the response to levodopa is often weak or short-lived. What matters is **how clearly, for how long, and which symptoms improve.**

## 5. Motor Fluctuations Become a Hallmark Feature in Parkinson's Disease

Living with Parkinson's disease over a long period, motor fluctuations such as wearing-off, delayed ON, ON–OFF fluctuation, and dyskinesia can appear. This also means that a neural circuit still exists that responds sufficiently to dopaminergic medication.

In parkinsonism that barely responds to levodopa, by contrast, this typical medication-response curve may not be clearly seen.

## 6. Looking at Red Flags

When distinguishing Parkinson's disease from atypical parkinsonism, clinicians look at certain **red flags**. These include early and repeated falls, rapid disease progression, severe early autonomic dysfunction, vertical gaze impairment, prominent cerebellar signs, early cognitive and behavioral change, and severe parkinsonism that shows little response to levodopa.

---

## Seeing Parkinsonism More Clearly Reveals Parkinson's Disease

As I studied the various forms of parkinsonism again, the features of Parkinson's disease itself came into sharper focus. Set against the other diseases, it becomes much clearer why Parkinson's disease so often begins on one side, why the response to levodopa matters, and why motor fluctuations become such an important issue over the long course of the disease.

> Parkinson's disease is a form of parkinsonism, but not all parkinsonism is Parkinson's disease.
