# Appendix 6. Parkinson's Disease and Parkinsonism

## — What Helps Tell Them Apart?

So far we have looked at Parkinson's disease alongside multiple system atrophy (MSA), progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), corticobasal syndrome (CBS), and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB). On the surface, they all look similar. Movement slows, the body stiffens, walking becomes difficult, and in some cases tremor appears as well.

But look closely, and each disease turns out to have **a different pathological protein, a different circuit that it mainly affects, and a different order in which symptoms appear.**

## The 8 Questions That Help Tell Them Apart

### 1. Response to Levodopa
Parkinson's disease tends to respond relatively well to levodopa, and as the disease progresses, motor fluctuations such as wearing-off and dyskinesia can appear. In MSA, PSP, and CBS, the response to levodopa is generally limited.

**"How clearly, and for how long, does levodopa work?"**

### 2. How the Disease Begins
Parkinson's disease commonly begins **asymmetrically**. CBS is also asymmetric, but cortical features such as apraxia, cortical sensory loss, and alien limb phenomenon appear together with it. In PSP, axial symptoms and postural instability can become prominent early. In MSA, autonomic problems or cerebellar symptoms can appear early. In DLB, cognitive fluctuations and visual hallucinations can appear together early on.

### 3. Early Falls
When falls occur repeatedly from a very early stage, especially with a **tendency to fall backward**, this makes one think of PSP.

### 4. Looking at the Eyes
**Vertical supranuclear gaze palsy**, especially prominent difficulty looking downward, strongly suggests PSP. "Can the eyes move up and down well?"

### 5. The Autonomic Nervous System
When **severe early orthostatic hypotension and rapidly progressive urinary dysfunction** appear together, early in the course of parkinsonism, this makes one think of MSA. What matters is not simply whether these symptoms are present, but how early and how severely they appear.

### 6. Cerebellar Symptoms
When **prominent cerebellar ataxia** (a wide-based gait, limb ataxia, dysarthria) appears together with parkinsonism, this makes one think of MSA-C.

### 7. Cortical Symptoms
When symptoms such as **apraxia, cortical sensory loss, alien limb phenomenon, and myoclonus** appear, this makes one think of CBS. In Parkinson's disease, the hand becomes slow; in CBS, the motor plan itself — knowing how to use the hand — can collapse.

### 8. The Timing of Cognitive Symptoms and Visual Hallucinations
When **cognitive fluctuations, recurrent and detailed visual hallucinations, and REM sleep behavior disorder** appear at nearly the same time as parkinsonism, or within a year of it, this suggests DLB.

## Comparing the Pathological Proteins

| Disease | Main pathology | Primary cell type |
|---|---|---|
| Parkinson's disease | α-synuclein | Neurons (Lewy bodies) |
| MSA | α-synuclein | Oligodendrocytes (GCI) |
| PSP | 4R tau | Neurons + astrocytes (tufted astrocytes) |
| CBS/CBD | 4R tau | Neurons + astrocytes (astrocytic plaques) |
| DLB | α-synuclein | Neurons (widespread, extending to cortex and limbic system) |

## In the End, Timing Is What Matters Most

The single most important word running through this entire appendix is **timing**.

Looking only at which symptoms are present is not enough. We also need to look at when they began, what appeared first, how quickly the disease progressed, and how it responded to medication.

- Falls can occur in any of these diseases. But when they occur **early**, this points more toward PSP.
- Autonomic symptoms also occur in several of these diseases. But when they are **very severe early on**, this points more toward MSA.
- Cognitive impairment also occurs in several of these diseases. But when it appears **at nearly the same time as motor symptoms**, this points more toward DLB.

> In the end, it comes back to neuroanatomy. **The names of the diseases differ, but the symptoms come from the circuits.** Which circuit breaks down first determines which symptom appears first.

---

**Appendix 6 in one line:** The key to telling Parkinson's disease apart from atypical parkinsonism is not any single symptom, but looking together at the response to levodopa, the speed of disease progression, and the order in which early falls, autonomic symptoms, eye-movement abnormalities, cerebellar signs, cortical signs, and cognitive symptoms appear.
