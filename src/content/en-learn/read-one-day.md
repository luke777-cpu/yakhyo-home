  - title: First, find the dose marker
    detail: It's the small triangle below the horizontal axis. The other three points are all measured as a distance from this one, so this is where to start.
  - title: Second, the point where the curve starts to rise
    detail: This is where the line lifts off the bottom. The horizontal distance from the dose marker to this point is the first value to look at.
  - title: Third, the highest point
    detail: How high it climbed, vertically. Rather than reading the number on the axis, compare it against other days and other doses.
  - title: Fourth, the point where it starts to come down
    detail: Look at how far ahead of the next dose marker it is. This is where the length of time it held up comes from.

    detail: Something that looks like just that day's circumstances when you look at only one day reveals itself, once a few days are gathered, as either a repeating pattern or not.
record:
  - title: The time of the dose
    detail: This is the first reference point. If it's uncertain, the other three points all become uncertain too.
  - title: The time you felt things start to change
    detail: This becomes the second point.
  - title: The best time, and your output then
    detail: This becomes the position and height of the third point.
  - title: The time things started to feel heavy again
    detail: This is the fourth point. Without it, the entire right side of the peak is left empty.
forVisit:
  - Which of the four points has changed recently
  - Whether that change repeats across several days
  - Whether it shows up only at a particular dose during the day
  - If there are stretches where the curve is empty, whether that just means there was no record for that time
faq:
  - q: The curve looks jagged — did I record something wrong?
    a: No. If the times you recorded are spread far apart, the curve is drawn that way too. As you record more often, it naturally smooths out.
  - q: Is it okay if the vertical axis has no numbers?
    a: Yes. It's enough to read whether it's higher or lower compared with other days and other doses. This isn't a lab value — it's a comparison within yourself.
  - q: Looking at just one day, I can't really tell.
    a: That's normal. A single day's curve carries a lot of that day's particular circumstances, so you usually need to line up three days to a week's worth before repeating patterns start to show.
  - q: What does it mean when there's an empty stretch in the curve?
    a: It usually means there was no record for that time. It's not that nothing was happening in the body — it's that nothing was written down.

A single curve holds four pieces of information: **when the dose was taken, when it started to rise, how high it went, and when it came back down.**

Being able to look at these four things separately is what makes the graph useful. A statement like "the medication worked well today" bundles all four together, so there's no way to tell apart what was good and what fell short.

Each of the four points leads to a different story. If the **rising point** was pushed later, you look at the gap between the dose and meals along with it; if the **peak** was low, you look at that day's conditions; if the **coming-down point** was early, you look at the relationship between how long it held and the interval between doses.

At first, just scanning through in this order is enough. After a few days of doing it, your eye starts going to those spots on its own.
