reasons:
  - title: Memory keeps only the strongest points
    detail: The hardest moment and the best moment stay with you, but the gentle stretch in between gets erased. Most of the day is that stretch.
  - title: The time of day disappears first
    detail: What happened may stay with you, but what time it happened blurs within a single day. Yet it is mostly the time that gives the curve its meaning.
  - title: Days blend into each other
    detail: After three days, what happened on Tuesday and what happened on Thursday become one blur. You can no longer tell a repeating pattern from a one-off.
  - title: The most recent days get exaggerated
    detail: If yesterday was hard, the whole past week can feel like it was hard. With a record, you can actually count how many days it really was.
record:
  - title: The time you took the dose
    detail: Log it right when you take it. Every other interval is measured as a distance from this time.
  - title: The time you felt your body change
    detail: Log both the moment it got better and the moment it got heavy again, and the day's rise and fall takes shape.
  - title: Roughly how it was at that moment
    detail: It doesn't need to be exact. Logging with the same rough scale over several days is worth more than one perfectly precise entry.
  - title: One line for a day that felt unusually different
    detail: Something like "didn't sleep well" or "was out for a long time" is enough to become a clue when you look back on that day later.
forVisit:
  - What pattern keeps repeating, and how many times out of how many days
  - What you've noticed changing recently, and since when
  - Which time of day is hardest
  - What time relationship that has to your doses
faq:
  - q: Do I have to do this every single day without missing one?
    a: No. Even if some days are missed, the days you do have still carry that much value. Keeping it up for even just the week before a visit already makes the conversation much more concrete.
  - q: What difference does keeping a record actually make?
    a: Saying "I've been a bit off lately" becomes saying "starting with the third dose, the peak drops by about half, and that happened four days this week." The amount of information you can get across in the same amount of time changes.
  - q: Can I look at the record and decide for myself what to change?
    a: Reading a curve and deciding what to change are different things. Decisions about the type, dose, or timing of medication are something to discuss with your treating clinician — the record's role is to make the material for that conversation accurate, and nothing more.

A clinic visit is short. In that short time, you have to convey the last two or three months, but **what memory hands you back is not a summary of that stretch — it's a handful of scenes.**

The figure above shows a single dose coming and going. It carries four pieces of information — when you took it, when it started to rise, how high it went, and when it came back down. A day later, **three of those times are usually the first to go**, and all that's left is "today was okay."

Keeping a record is about holding on to those four things. It isn't a big undertaking — just **one tap when you take the dose, and one tap when you feel a change.**
