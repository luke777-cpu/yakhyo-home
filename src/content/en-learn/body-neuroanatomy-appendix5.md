# Appendix 5. Dementia with Lewy Bodies (DLB)

## — The same α-synuclein, so why do cognition and perception falter first?

Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies share an important biological feature: **α-synuclein** pathology.

Both can show Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites. Both can produce parkinsonism.

Yet the clinical pictures are not identical.

In Parkinson's disease, bradykinesia, rigidity, and tremor often dominate first. In **dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB)**, cognitive changes, fluctuations in attention and alertness, visual hallucinations, and REM sleep behavior disorder may become prominent early.

The difference is not simply the protein. It is also **which networks are involved, how widely, and in what order**.

## 1. What is DLB?

DLB is a major neurodegenerative cause of dementia, but its clinical pattern differs from a simple picture of steadily progressive memory loss.

Attention, alertness, visuospatial function, executive function, perception, sleep, and movement may all be involved.

A family may describe a person as “almost themselves” at one time and strikingly confused or disengaged a few hours later.

That **fluctuation** is important.

## 2. Cognition can fluctuate within the same day

A person may converse normally for a period, understand the situation, and then become blank, inattentive, or difficult to engage later the same day.

These **cognitive fluctuations** are one of the core clinical features of DLB.

They suggest instability in networks that maintain attention and arousal, not simply memory storage failure.

## 3. Why are visual hallucinations so characteristic?

DLB often produces **recurrent, well-formed visual hallucinations**: people, children, animals, or other clearly formed figures.

Seeing is not something the eyes do alone. Visual cortex, object-recognition systems, spatial networks, attention, memory, and arousal all contribute to perception.

When those networks become unstable, incomplete or internally generated information may be experienced as an external visual event.

So hallucinations in DLB are better understood as a **network-level disorder of perception**.

## 4. Why REM sleep behavior disorder matters

**REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD)** is a core feature of DLB.

During normal REM sleep, most skeletal muscles are effectively paralyzed. In RBD, that REM atonia is lost, so dreams may be enacted with shouting, punching, kicking, or falling from bed.

RBD can begin **years before** dementia or parkinsonism.

But other disorders — such as obstructive sleep apnea, confusional arousals, or periodic limb movements — can mimic dream enactment, so polysomnography may be needed when the diagnosis is uncertain.

## 5. Parkinsonism also occurs

DLB can produce bradykinesia, rigidity, gait dysfunction, and postural instability.

Levodopa may improve parkinsonism, but the response may be less robust than in typical Parkinson's disease.

There is also a clinical tradeoff: increasing dopaminergic therapy for movement can sometimes worsen hallucinations or confusion.

Treatment therefore requires balancing **motor benefit against cognitive and psychiatric stability**.

## 6. The difficult question — DLB or Parkinson's disease dementia?

Patients with long-standing Parkinson's disease can develop dementia. This is called **Parkinson's disease dementia (PDD)**.

DLB and PDD overlap substantially in pathology. Both are Lewy body diseases driven by neuronal α-synuclein pathology.

The practical clinical distinction therefore relies heavily on **timing**.

## 7. The one-year rule

When dementia precedes parkinsonism or develops at approximately the same time, the diagnosis generally favors DLB.

When a research distinction is required, the traditional **one-year rule** is still used: dementia beginning within one year of parkinsonism favors DLB, whereas dementia developing after well-established Parkinson's disease favors PDD.

This does **not** mean the biology suddenly changes at 12 months.

It is a practical clinical convention for separating two closely related Lewy body disease presentations.

## 8. The pathology is again α-synuclein

DLB is an **α-synucleinopathy**.

α-synuclein accumulates in neurons, forming Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites.

The difference from typical motor-first Parkinson's disease is not a completely different molecule, but the **distribution and timing of network involvement**, with limbic and cortical systems often clinically prominent early in DLB.

## 9. Same protein, different distribution — and different cells in MSA

This appendix series keeps returning to the same lesson.

Parkinson's disease and DLB are dominated by **neuronal** Lewy pathology.

MSA is also an α-synucleinopathy, but its defining pathology is predominantly **oligodendroglial GCI**.

The protein matters, but so do **cell type, anatomical distribution, and disease sequence**.

## 10. Acetylcholine matters too

DLB is not only a dopamine disorder.

Cholinergic systems, including basal forebrain networks, are important for attention, cognition, and visual processing and can be substantially affected.

This helps explain why **cholinesterase inhibitors** can be useful for cognitive and neuropsychiatric symptoms in some patients with DLB.

## 11. Autonomic symptoms are common

Orthostatic hypotension, constipation, urinary dysfunction, sweating abnormalities, and other autonomic symptoms can occur.

This overlaps with Parkinson's disease and MSA.

Again, the distinction depends less on whether a symptom exists than on **how early, how severe, and what else appears with it**.

## 12. Why antipsychotic sensitivity matters

DLB can be associated with severe sensitivity to dopamine-blocking **antipsychotic medications**.

A drug given to suppress hallucinations or delusions can sometimes produce marked worsening of rigidity, bradykinesia, alertness, or overall medical status.

That is why treatment of psychosis in DLB requires particular caution.

## 13. Hallucinations alone do not equal DLB

Visual hallucinations can also occur in Parkinson's disease, delirium, infection, sleep deprivation, medication toxicity, severe visual impairment, and other conditions.

So hallucination alone is not enough.

The full pattern matters: **cognitive fluctuation, recurrent well-formed hallucinations, RBD, parkinsonism, autonomic symptoms, and timing**.

## 14. Alzheimer pathology can coexist

Real brains do not always contain one neat pathology.

Many patients with DLB also have **amyloid-β and tau pathology** associated with Alzheimer disease.

This mixed pathology may influence the cognitive profile and rate of progression.

Neurodegenerative disease in older adults is often biologically mixed rather than textbook-pure.

## 15. What can biomarkers and imaging contribute?

MRI is useful for excluding structural disease but does not diagnose DLB by itself.

Supportive or indicative tests can include **dopamine transporter imaging**, **cardiac MIBG scintigraphy**, and polysomnographic demonstration of **REM sleep without atonia**.

The diagnosis still depends heavily on the clinical pattern and its time course.

## 16. DLB shows why timing matters

DLB and PDD may be biologically close, yet the clinical name changes according to the order in which cognitive and motor symptoms appear.

The same hallucination means something different when it appears before parkinsonism, alongside it, or after many years of established Parkinson's disease.

In neurodegenerative disease, **what happened first can be as important as what is present now**.

---

## DLB and Parkinson's disease through the lens of pathology

Parkinson's disease and DLB are both neuronal α-synucleinopathies.

Motor-first Parkinson's disease often brings nigrostriatal dysfunction to the foreground. DLB brings cortical, limbic, attention, and perceptual networks to the foreground earlier.

> The order in which α-synuclein pathology affects networks helps shape the order in which symptoms appear.
