# Appendix 5. Dementia with Lewy Bodies (DLB)

## — Same α-synuclein, so why do memory and perception falter first?

Study Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies side by side, and something strange turns up. In both, **α-synuclein** is the core pathological protein. In both, Lewy bodies can appear. And yet the clinical picture is not the same.

In Parkinson's disease, motor symptoms such as bradykinesia, rigidity, and tremor usually come to the front first. In dementia with Lewy bodies, or DLB, changes in cognitive function, severe fluctuations in attention, vivid visual hallucinations, and REM sleep behavior disorder can become prominent early on instead.

The answer, again, can be found in **where the pathology has spread, and how much**.

## 1. The core of DLB — cognitive fluctuations and hallucinations

DLB can present a somewhat different picture. At one time of day, conversation flows well. Yet a few hours later, the person can become blank, unable to concentrate, unable to answer questions properly. This may be **because the neural network that maintains attention and arousal itself can become unstable** in DLB.

**Recurrent, well-formed visual hallucinations** also matter a great deal. People sometimes see concrete forms — people, children, animals. This is not simply a problem with the eyes; it is a problem of the entire visual-processing and attention network. The brain may misinterpret incomplete visual information, or may accept an internally generated image as real.

## 2. Why REM sleep behavior disorder matters

**REM sleep behavior disorder** is commonly seen alongside DLB. Behavior from within a dream can play out in the actual body — shouting, flailing the arms, kicking.

What is interesting is that this symptom can appear far earlier than cognitive impairment or parkinsonism. This may suggest that α-synuclein pathology can invade the **brainstem's sleep-regulating circuitry** before it reaches the motor cortex.

## 3. DLB vs. PDD — the one-year rule

Patients with Parkinson's disease can also develop dementia after a long time has passed. This is called **Parkinson's disease dementia, or PDD**. The pathology of DLB and PDD overlaps substantially — in both, α-synuclein and Lewy body and Lewy neurite pathology matter.

Clinically, the two are distinguished mainly by **timing**.

If cognitive impairment appears before Parkinsonian motor symptoms, or emerges clearly within **one year** of their onset, DLB is considered clinically. If, instead, Parkinson's disease has been clearly present for several years before dementia develops, it is called PDD. This is **the one-year rule**.

## 4. Pathology — same protein, different distribution

Early in typical Parkinson's disease, pathology in the substantia nigra and brainstem plays the major role in producing motor symptoms. In DLB, the pathology is often spread extensively, reaching the limbic system and neocortex as well.

> What decides the disease is not only which protein is involved. **Which cells, which brain regions, and how widely it spreads** matter together, just as much.

## 5. Why hypersensitivity to antipsychotic medications occurs

One critically important clinical issue in DLB is **hypersensitivity to antipsychotic medications**. If visual hallucinations or delusions are present and a medication that strongly blocks dopamine receptors is used because of them, rigidity and bradykinesia can worsen sharply, and overall clinical status can deteriorate significantly, in some cases.

---

## Understanding DLB brings Parkinson's disease into sharper focus

Comparing Parkinson's disease with DLB lets us see Parkinson's disease a little differently. Parkinson's disease often begins with movement, but it is by no means a disease confined only to the motor circuit.

> Which circuit α-synuclein pathology begins in, and in what order it spreads — that order is what shapes the order in which a patient's symptoms appear.
