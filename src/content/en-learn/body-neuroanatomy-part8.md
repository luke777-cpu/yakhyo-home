# Part 8. Parkinson's Disease Is Not Only a Movement Disorder

## — Why can it affect sleep, the gut, bladder, mood, and cognition?

When people hear "Parkinson's disease," the first thing that comes to mind is movement. The hand tremors. The body stiffens. Movements slow down. Steps shorten, and the first step is hard to take.

For that reason, Parkinson's disease has long been described as **a disease of movement.**

That description is not wrong. But if you look closely at Parkinson's disease over time, there are far too many symptoms that cannot be explained by movement alone. Sleep changes. The sense of smell may weaken. Constipation appears. Blood pressure becomes unstable. Mood and motivation can change. The speed of thought and attention can also be affected.

So the question changes. **Is Parkinson's disease really only a disease of movement?**

## Circuits involved, at a glance

All of these circuits exist at once, in a single patient.

| Symptom | Circuits mainly involved |
|---|---|
| Sleep (REM sleep behavior disorder) | Brainstem sleep circuit |
| Reduced sense of smell | Olfactory system (may appear before motor symptoms) |
| Constipation / reduced bowel motility | Autonomic nervous system, enteric nervous system |
| Orthostatic hypotension | Sympathetic autonomic nervous system |
| Bladder dysfunction | Basal ganglia, brainstem, autonomic nervous system |
| Pain / fatigue | Central pain circuits, multiple systems |
| Depression / anxiety | Limbic system, serotonin, noradrenaline |
| Cognitive / attention decline | Prefrontal–basal ganglia cognitive circuit |
| Impulse control problems | Dopamine reward circuit (VTA → frontal lobe) |
| Visual hallucinations | Visual cortex, acetylcholine, sleep system |

## 1. Looking only at the substantia nigra shows only half of Parkinson's disease

The changes of Parkinson's disease are not limited to the substantia nigra. Various regions may be affected, including several brainstem nuclei, the autonomic nervous system, the olfactory system, the limbic system, the cerebral cortex, and even the peripheral autonomic nervous system.

And it is not only dopamine that changes. Other neurotransmitter systems are also involved, including noradrenaline, serotonin, and acetylcholine.

## 2. It may begin before motor symptoms appear

Some non-motor symptoms **may appear years before** the typical motor symptoms such as hand tremor or bradykinesia. The best-known examples are reduced sense of smell, constipation, and REM sleep behavior disorder.

This carries an important meaning. It suggests that **the pathological process of Parkinson's disease may not have suddenly begun on the day the hand started to tremble.**

## 3. The gut is part of the medication response curve

Constipation is very common in patients with Parkinson's disease. But it is not simply a problem caused by reduced movement. Gastrointestinal motility is regulated by the autonomic nervous system and the enteric nervous system, and in Parkinson's disease these systems can also be affected.

Here another important issue arises. **The gut is the passage levodopa must cross to be absorbed.** Even after the medication is swallowed, if it lingers in the stomach, it reaches the small intestine later. That delays the onset of the drug's effect as well.

> This is one of the reasons the same medication, taken at the same time, can work quickly on some days and slowly on others.

**The gut sits at the front end of the medication response curve.**

## 4. Why protein is related to medication response

Levodopa is structurally related to amino acids. In the process of being absorbed in the gut and crossing the blood–brain barrier, it shares transporters with large neutral amino acids. As a result, in some patients, when a protein-rich meal overlaps with the timing of a levodopa dose, the medication's effect may be delayed or weakened.

This, too, shows something important. In Parkinson's disease, medication response is **not only a matter of the brain.** It is the result of an entire process that begins in the gastrointestinal tract and continues through the blood to the brain.

## 5. Parkinson's disease is present even while asleep

In Parkinson's disease, several sleep problems can appear, including difficulty falling asleep, frequent awakenings, nighttime movement problems, and daytime sleepiness.

Among these, **REM sleep behavior disorder** has drawn particular attention. In normal REM sleep, even while dreaming, most skeletal muscles are suppressed so that the dream does not turn into actual movement. But when this suppression system does not work properly, the actions within a dream can be expressed through the body — shouting, flailing the arms, or kicking.

This phenomenon shows that the brainstem's sleep-regulating circuit may be related to Parkinson's disease.

## 6. Depression and anxiety are not only matters of the mind

Anyone living with a chronic illness for a long time can become depressed or anxious. So it is not right to explain all depression and anxiety in Parkinson's disease patients purely as brain pathology. But it is also not enough to view it only as "being upset because of the illness."

That is because the neurotransmitter systems involved in emotion and motivation — dopamine, serotonin, noradrenaline — can themselves be affected in Parkinson's disease. In other words, the mood changes of Parkinson's disease may arise from an overlap between the psychological experience of living with the illness and changes in the nervous system itself.

## 7. Dopamine is not only in charge of movement

There is not just one dopamine circuit. The nigrostriatal pathway is closely tied to movement, but other dopamine pathways are also involved in reward, motivation, attention, and behavioral choice.

So Parkinson's disease and its treatment can affect behaviors beyond movement as well. In particular, some dopaminergic medications may be related to impulse control problems in susceptible patients. This should not simply be interpreted as "weakened willpower." It is because the neural circuits that choose behavior and evaluate reward are themselves influenced by dopamine.

## Non-motor symptoms, understood through the body

Before I studied neuroanatomy again, I evaluated Parkinson's disease mainly through movement. Did I walk well today? Is my body stiff? Am I in OFF or ON? Is there dyskinesia?

But over time, I came to understand that motor function alone does not determine my day. How I slept, how my gut is moving, how my blood pressure and fatigue feel, how my concentration and mood are — all of these together make up the body of that day.

> To understand Parkinson's disease, you cannot look only at the hands and feet. You also have to look at the sleeping brain, at the gut, at the autonomic nervous system, and at the circuits that create emotion and thought.

Parkinson's disease is a disease of movement, but it is not only a disease of movement. It may in fact be one of the clearest illustrations that many neural systems are connected to one another.

---

**Part 8 in one line:** Parkinson's disease can be explained as starting with the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra, but it should be understood as a body-wide neurological disease in which the symptoms patients actually experience arise from changes across many neural networks — not only the motor circuit, but also the autonomic nervous system, sleep, emotion, cognition, and sensation.
