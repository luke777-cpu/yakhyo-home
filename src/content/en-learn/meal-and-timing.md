reasons:
  - title: The speed of emptying from the stomach into the small intestine
    detail: Medication is absorbed in the small intestine, not the stomach. If a meal is still sitting in the stomach, it can take longer for the medication to reach the small intestine.
  - title: Sharing the same pathway as protein
    detail: Levodopa is absorbed through the same pathway as amino acids. When the timing overlaps with a meal high in protein, the amount absorbed is sometimes said to vary from day to day.
  - title: Fatty meals and large meals
    detail: How fast the stomach empties also depends on the amount and type of the meal. Even at the same time of day, a different meal means different conditions.
  - title: How much this varies from person to person
    detail: In some people the effect of meals shows up strongly; in others it is barely noticeable. Which is true for you can only be known from a record.
record:
  - title: The actual time the dose was taken
    detail: Noting it at the moment, rather than from memory, is what makes the gap accurate.
  - title: The start time of meals before and after
    detail: 'What matters is not "after eating" but the actual clock time, so the gap can be calculated.'
  - title: Whether that meal included meat, fish, eggs, or dairy
    detail: Detailed nutrition information is not needed. Knowing roughly whether the meal was high in protein or not is enough.
  - title: The time you felt the change begin
    detail: For seeing the effect of a meal, this moment shows more than the peak does.
  - title: How the same dose went over several days
    detail: A single day cannot tell you whether the meal was the cause or something else about that day was.

Both curves show a dose taken at the same time. The only thing that differs is that day's meal. Yet the solid line stays much lower for a while before it rises late, and even its peak does not reach as high as the dotted line.

What matters here is not concluding **"it was because of the meal."** A single curve cannot tell you that. Sleep may have been short that same day, or the stomach may have felt off since the day before.

Instead, what this article suggests is much simpler: **write down the dose time and the meal time side by side, for just a few days.** Only that way can you see whether a day with a thirty-minute gap and a day with a two-hour gap actually produce different shapes, or whether there is not much difference at all.

Once a few days have built up, one of two things usually shows. If the curve is pushed back at the same point on every day with a short gap, that is a pattern worth bringing up at a clinic visit. If it is uneven regardless of the gap, the cause is more likely to lie elsewhere. Either way, you end up able to describe things far more specifically than without any record at all.
