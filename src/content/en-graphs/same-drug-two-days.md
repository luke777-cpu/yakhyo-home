If you keep records for a few days, you're bound to run into this question. **"I took the same medication at the same time — so why is it different day to day?"**

The graph above draws that situation exactly as it happens. The dosing times on the two days are identical. But **the first dose shows a big difference between the two curves** — one rose early and climbed high, while the other didn't rise until much later and stopped at about half that height. The **second dose, on the other hand, shows the two curves nearly overlapping.**

There are three things to read in this graph.

First, **being different from day to day isn't strange.** The conditions that affect absorption shift a little every day, and the curve simply shows that.

Second, **not every dose of the day differs equally.** Some doses show a wide gap, and others are nearly identical. So saying "my medication isn't working well these days" is much less accurate than saying "my morning dose varies a lot from day to day."

Third, **neither curve is the baseline.** The two lines in the graph above aren't normal and abnormal — they're just two different days. That's why this site draws both days in the same color and separates them only by a solid line and a dashed line. Drawing one of them in a faded color would make that day look like it was wrong.

Memory alone can't make this comparison. You remember that one day was good and another was bad, but you don't remember **what time it changed and by how much.** With a few days of records on hand, laying two days side by side is enough to answer that question.
